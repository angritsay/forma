Home CrossFit equipment: what to buy first, what to buy later and what to skip

Home CrossFit equipment: what to buy first is the question that stalls more beginners than any workout does. While you compare racks, barbells and rowing machines, a month goes by without a single session. Coaching people who train in apartments taught me the opposite lesson: the fastest progress comes from those who start with a mat and a chair and add iron once their technique is solid. This guide is an honest look at what you actually need for CrossFit at home, in what order to buy it, which weights to pick and what each Forma course requires. No brands and no “mandatory” ten-item list.

What you will get from this guide

  • Four tiers of a home setup, from nothing to complete, and the order to move through them.
  • How to choose dumbbells and a kettlebell by type and weight when space and budget are limited.
  • What each course needs and how the app adapts the program to the gear you own.
  • A workout on the minimal setup, buying mistakes and the rule that tells you when to add the next item.

Home CrossFit equipment: what to buy and in what order

CrossFit is not a list of implements; it is a list of movement patterns: squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, jump and trunk work. Any home gym equipment for CrossFit should cover those patterns rather than fill a corner of the room. The good news is that the first four patterns are covered by your bodyweight, a chair and the floor. Iron adds load when bodyweight is no longer enough; it does not replace technique.

TierWhat it includesWhat it unlocks
0Mat, sturdy chairSquats, incline push-ups, step-ups, planks, stretching
1Jump rope, resistance bandsJumping, rows, shoulder warm-up
2A pair of dumbbells or one kettlebellLoaded squats and hinges, presses, swings, carries
3Pull-up bar, plyo boxPull-ups, hangs, higher step-ups

Tier 0 costs nothing and leaves no excuses. Tiers 1 to 3 are the order I recommend when someone asks for a home workout equipment list and does not know where to start.

Tier 0: a mat and a chair

The mat is not decoration; it is for your knees, elbows and spine. Cat-cow, child’s pose, planks and sit-ups on bare laminate kill the habit fast. Six to eight millimetres thick is the compromise between comfort and stability in a squat; very soft yoga mats are a poor base for loaded work.

The chair is the most underrated piece of gear. You do incline push-ups off it, step-ups onto it and bench dips from it. The requirements are simple: no wheels, no soft cushion, no higher than your knee, and it must not slide when you lean your full weight on it. A stool, the bottom stair or a solid bench does the same job.

Dumbbells or a kettlebell: which comes first

This is the main tier-2 question, and the answer depends on what you want. Dumbbells give more options for strength and muscle: floor press, bent-over rows, lunges with two loads. A kettlebell teaches the hinge and the swing better and delivers conditioning with one lump of iron. If you are torn, decide like this: if you want to get stronger and build your body first, dumbbells; if you want breathing, back and glutes with minimal space, a kettlebell.

What dumbbells to buy for home

Two types: fixed pairs and adjustable. A fixed pair is simple and needs no maintenance, but your legs and back are stronger than your shoulders: a weight that is heavy for a standing press is light for a deadlift. So either two pairs, or adjustable dumbbells with plates, or one pair and difficulty managed through tempo and pauses.

The rule for the light pair: you can press it overhead cleanly 15 times in a row. For the heavy pair: eight deadlifts take real effort. For most beginners that lands roughly between 4 and 10 kg per hand for the light pair and 12 to 20 kg for the heavy one; the exact number depends on your bodyweight and history, not on your age or sex. If you own a single pair, pick the one that makes 12 goblet squats honestly hard; in the presses you compensate with tempo. Weight selection for every movement is covered in the full body dumbbell workout at home.

Which kettlebell to buy

One bell is enough for a full course: swings, goblet squats, the standing press, the Turkish get-up and carries. The weight guideline is the same one the Forma course uses: men with some training experience 16 kg, without it 12 kg; women 8 to 12 kg. A bell you cannot swing cleanly ten times is too heavy to start with; one that leaves your heart rate flat after twenty swings is too light.

Two body types: cast iron (the size grows with the weight) and competition steel (the same size at every weight). Both work at home; the handle matters more: smooth, no seam or burrs, wide enough for a two-hand grip in the swing. Rough paint or a rubber coating gets in the way when the bell has to rotate in your hand during cleans and snatches.

Jump rope, bands, pull-up bar and box

Jump rope is the cheapest way to add jumping: single-unders and later double-unders. Length: stand on the middle of the rope; the handles should reach your armpits. A thin coated steel cable spins faster than a fabric rope but needs ceiling height and does not like concrete.

Resistance bands cover the pulling you cannot do without a bar: the band row and the band pull-apart for the shoulders. A set of two or three resistances handles both the warm-up and the strength work.

Pull-up bar unlocks vertical pulling: dead hangs, negative pull-ups, pull-ups and hanging knee raises. A telescopic doorway bar holds by pressure and needs checking before every set; a wall-mounted bar is safer but has to go into brick or concrete, not plasterboard.

Box is for step-ups and jumps at 40 to 50 cm. At home it is a tier-3 item, because a chair and a stair cover the same job at the start.

What the Forma course needs

Right now Forma has one course open — Start: home CrossFit basics — and it needs only tier 1: a mat and a sturdy chair. You do dips off the chair and step-ups onto it, and everything else happens on the mat. The dumbbells, kettlebell, rope and bar from this guide come later, once the base is there and you want to add weight.

Between sessions you need no gear at all: the Small Steps Club in the app gives you one small task a day — a walk, twenty squats, a glass of water before coffee — and it gets done wherever you are standing.

How the app works with your gear. In your profile you tick what you own and enter the weights of your dumbbells and bells. Loads in the programs are labelled light, medium and heavy, and Forma maps them to your implements: with a single bell all three labels point to it and the volume and rest adjust instead. No rope means rope jumps become jumping jacks and the program stays the same. And the chair can always be swapped for squats or reverse lunges when nothing sturdy is at hand.

A workout on the minimal setup

A minimal home gym is a chair, a mat and one implement: a kettlebell or a pair of dumbbells. About 30 minutes.

Warm-up, two rounds. Jog in place 45 seconds, arm circles 30 seconds, squat to stand 6 reps, glute bridge 12 reps.

EMOM 16 minutes, four stations in rotation. Minute 1: 12 goblet squats with the bell or one dumbbell. Minute 2: 10 push-ups. Minute 3: 15 kettlebell swings or 12 dumbbell deadlifts. Minute 4: 8 step-ups onto the chair per leg. Four rounds; each task should take no more than 40 seconds, and the rest of the minute is rest.

Finisher. Three sets of farmer carry, 40 seconds each with the heaviest thing you own, 30 seconds of rest.

Cool-down. Hamstring stretch and hip flexor stretch, 30 seconds per side, child’s pose 45 seconds.

How the app scales this session. Before you start, Forma offers “easier”, “as planned” or “harder”: the first trims the reps by about 15 percent and lengthens the rest, the third adds volume and shortens the breaks. If your test placed you at level 1, push-ups become knee push-ups and swings become kettlebell deadlifts. After the session you rate the effort from 1 to 10, and the next one adjusts.

Common buying mistakes

  • Dumbbells that are too light. A 2 kg pair is fine for warming up the shoulders and nothing else. Squats and hinges need a weight you could not curl with one arm.
  • A kettlebell “to grow into”. The first weeks are about learning the hinge; a 24 kg bell in a beginner’s hands turns the swing into a back lift.
  • A full home gym on day one. A rack, a barbell and a rower before you train three times a week consistently are furniture, not equipment.
  • A doorway bar without a test. A hollow door frame and an old telescopic bar are a common cause of falls. Load the bar with your feet still on the floor before you hang from it.
  • No mat on a hard floor. A saving that ends with sore knees in lunges and skipped planks.
  • Buying instead of training. If you did fewer than eight sessions last month, a new implement changes nothing. Habit first, iron second.

If you have joint, back or blood-pressure problems, talk to a doctor before your first loaded work and start with the lightest implements.

How to expand the setup

  1. Weeks 1 to 4. Mat and chair. Do four weeks of CrossFit without equipment or the Start course: squat, hinge, push-up and plank technique should be solid before the first piece of iron.
  2. Month 2. One implement: a kettlebell or a pair of dumbbells by the rules above. One, not two: you do not yet know which you prefer.
  3. Month 3. A jump rope and bands. They are cheap and close the gaps in jumping and pulling.
  4. Later. A pull-up bar, a second pair or a second bell, a box, as the exercises with your current set become easy.

The signal that it is time to buy the next item: two consecutive sessions rated 6 out of 10 or lower with the full plan completed, the same rule Forma uses to recommend “harder”. Until that signal shows up, there is nothing to buy. What to do with the bell once it arrives is covered in kettlebell workouts at home for beginners.

Wrap-up

Home CrossFit equipment builds in tiers, and the first tier is already in your room. A mat and a chair cover a month of technique, a kettlebell or dumbbells cover the next six months of strength, and a rope, bands and a bar close the remaining patterns. If you want to start today rather than wait for a delivery, open the Start course: twenty workouts on a mat and a chair with no other gear, and by the end of it you will know exactly what to buy.

FAQ

What should I buy first for CrossFit at home? +

A mat and a sturdy chair: they cover the first month of technique work. The first loaded implement, a kettlebell or a pair of dumbbells, is worth buying once bodyweight exercises have become easy.

Dumbbells or a kettlebell: which is better for home? +

Dumbbells give more options for strength and muscle; a kettlebell teaches the hinge and the swing better and takes less space. One of the two is enough to start; buy the other once you know what you enjoy.

What dumbbell weight should I buy for home workouts? +

A light pair is one you can press overhead cleanly 15 times in a row; a heavy pair is one that makes eight deadlifts a real effort. Adjustable dumbbells or two fixed pairs are the most practical.

Which kettlebell weight is right for a beginner? +

Men with some training experience 16 kg, without it 12 kg; women 8 to 12 kg. The handle should be smooth, without a seam, and wide enough for a two-hand grip.

Do I need a pull-up bar for CrossFit at home? +

Not at the start. Resistance bands cover pulling in the first months; a bar makes sense once you do push-ups and planks confidently and are ready to learn hangs and negative pull-ups.
