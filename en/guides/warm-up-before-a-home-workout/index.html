Warm-up before a home workout: 7 minutes that protect your knees, shoulders and lower back

Warm up before a home workout the right way: a 7-minute routine from the exercise library, what to prep before squats, push-ups and jumps. Read and try it.

Warm up before a home workout and the first set stops being the most dangerous one. Those seven to ten minutes are what most people skip because “there is not enough time anyway”, and they are also what decides how the squats and push-ups go today and how your knees, shoulders and lower back feel tomorrow. A warm-up does not “warm the muscles” in the kitchen sense: it raises your heart rate and tissue temperature, takes every joint through its working range, and reminds the nervous system of the movements that are about to be loaded. Below is a ready 7-minute routine from the Forma exercise library, what to change before different sessions, and what a warm-up should never include.

Why warm up before a home workout at all

The ACSM guidelines for exercise testing and prescription describe a warm-up as 5–10 minutes of light-to-moderate cardiorespiratory and muscular activity before the main part. The reasons are practical.

  • Temperature and blood flow. A warm muscle stretches further and contracts faster, joint fluid gets less viscous, and the first squats stop creaking.
  • Range of motion. At home you have most likely been sitting for hours before the session. Hip flexors are short, the upper back is stiff, and without preparation the squat is shallower and the push-up sags.
  • The nervous system. A warm-up before strength training is a rehearsal: light versions of the working movements switch on the right muscles, so the first working set becomes ordinary instead of the riskiest set of the day.
  • Heart rate. Going from resting pulse straight into burpees is a poor idea for the heart and for your head. A gradual rise over a couple of minutes is far easier to tolerate. If you have high blood pressure, a heart condition or get dizzy under exertion, talk to a doctor before you start training.

The three layers of a warm-up

A good warm-up before CrossFit, or any home session, is built from three layers.

  1. Pulse: 1–2 minutes. Jogging in place, jumping jacks, easy rope skips. The goal is to get warm and breathing, nothing more. RPE 3–4.
  2. Joints and range: 3–4 minutes. A joint mobility warm-up means dynamic movement through full range: arm circles, leg swings, cat-cow, a lunge with rotation. Not static stretching: long holds of 30–60 seconds before strength work can briefly reduce force and power output, so they belong in the cool-down.
  3. Specific part: 2–3 minutes. Light versions of what is in the workout: bodyweight squats before squats, incline push-ups before push-ups, easy kettlebell deadlifts before swings.

About seven minutes with transitions. On a no-jump day, sore knees or the first two weeks of a course, swap the jumping jacks for high knees done as a march, feet staying on the floor.

The specific part, by session

  • Squat and press day. 10 air squats and 5 incline push-ups, two rounds.
  • Hinge and core day. 8 bird dogs and 8 dead bugs; the core wakes up before the lower back gets loaded.
  • Jumps and burpees. 10 slow half burpees, then 5 low jump squats with soft landings.
  • Dumbbells and kettlebell. Kettlebell halos, 5 each way, and 15 band pull-aparts get the shoulders ready for presses and snatches. Before swings, 10 kettlebell deadlifts with no speed.

How the app handles it. In Forma’s courses the warm-up and cool-down are separate blocks at the start and end of every session, and the app does not scale their volume: “easier” and “harder” change the reps in the working blocks and the length of the rest pauses, while the warm-up reps and seconds stay exactly as the coach wrote them. That is deliberate; the warm-up needs to be the same at every level. Limitations still apply here: if you flagged wrist problems, hand-supported floor work such as planks and push-ups is swapped for gentler variants where one exists, and with hypertension the squat hold and the plank are capped at 30 seconds.

The cool-down after the workout

The cool-down after a workout mirrors the warm-up, and this is where static stretching belongs. Two or three minutes of walking in place until your pulse drops to a talking pace, then 30–45 seconds per side of the hip flexor stretch and the hamstring stretch, finishing in child’s pose with slow breathing. A cool-down does not cancel soreness, but it brings you back to a calm state and locks in the range you have just worked through.

Common mistakes

  • Static stretching instead of a warm-up. Half a minute in a deep split before squats makes the legs feel weaker, not stronger. Dynamic before, static after.
  • A warm-up that tires you. If you are already at RPE 6 when the warm-up ends, you have spent resources the workout needed. The pulse should rise, but you should still be able to talk.
  • The same warm-up for everything. A press day needs shoulders and upper back; a jump day needs ankles and soft landings. The specific part changes.
  • Skipping it when short on time. If you have 20 minutes, the warm-up takes five of them, not zero. A shorter main part beats cold knees. How to fit everything into a short session is covered in the guide to CrossFit without equipment.
  • Making it too long. Fifteen minutes of mobility before a 20-minute workout is out of proportion. Seven to ten minutes is enough.

How to progress

  1. Weeks 1–2. Do the full routine from the table, no shortcuts. Notice which joints complain the most.
  2. Weeks 3–4. Add the specific part for the day: two rounds of light working movements. The warm-up grows to 8–9 minutes.
  3. Month 2. Go deeper on the problem areas: two extra minutes on the ankles before jumps or on the upper back before presses. Drop what already comes easily.
  4. The sign of progress: the first working set feels like the second, not the hardest, and your squat depth in the hold has improved without effort.

If you are just starting, read the beginner’s guide to CrossFit at home: the warm-up is built into every session of the first four weeks. For rest days, and why a walk recovers you better than the couch, see the guide to rest days and steps.

Wrap-up

A warm-up before a home workout is seven minutes that turn the first set from the riskiest into an ordinary one: pulse, joints, a rehearsal of the working movements. Dynamic before, static after, and a specific part for whatever is on today’s plan. FAQ

How long should a warm-up before a home workout be? +

Seven to ten minutes: one or two on pulse, three or four on joints and range, two or three on light versions of the working movements. Before a 20-minute session, five minutes is enough.

Should I stretch before a workout? +

Dynamically, yes: leg swings, arm circles, lunges with rotation. Long static holds before strength work can briefly reduce force output, so keep those for the cool-down.

Do I need a warm-up before a short workout? +

Yes, especially if it includes jumps or burpees. Five minutes is the minimum that protects your knees and shoulders; shorten the main part rather than start cold.

What is the difference between a warm-up and a cool-down? +

A warm-up raises your pulse and prepares the joints through movement; a cool-down brings the pulse down and locks in range with static stretching. The order never changes: dynamic before, static after.

What should I warm up before a kettlebell workout? +

Shoulders and upper back (kettlebell halos, arm circles), hips (leg swings, glute bridges) and a few easy kettlebell deadlifts before swings.
