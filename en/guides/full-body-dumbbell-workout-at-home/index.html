Full body dumbbell workout at home: a coach's plan for the whole body

A full body dumbbell workout at home: which exercises to pick, how to pick the weight, sets, reps and rest, and how to progress. Read the plan and start today.

A full body dumbbell workout at home is the shortest route from “I move a bit” to “I am getting stronger”. One pair of dumbbells covers every basic movement: squat, deadlift, press, row, lunge and carry. You do not depend on a gym, the session fits in 35 to 40 minutes, and the load can be measured rather than guessed. Below is the program I would give someone with one or two pairs of dumbbells and a mat: how to choose the weight, the full session with reps and rest, the mistakes I correct most often, and a plain progression scheme for the first two months.

  • How to choose the dumbbell weight for each movement when you only own one pair.

Why one pair of dumbbells covers the whole body

Strength training at home is built on movement patterns, not on how much equipment you own. Five patterns, the squat, the hinge, the horizontal and vertical press, the row and the carry, load every major muscle group. Dumbbells are convenient because each of those movements works with them without extra technique: there is no bar to balance on your back and no kettlebell to catch.

The WHO advises adults to get 150 to 300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity a week plus muscle-strengthening work for the major muscle groups on two or more days a week. The ACSM says the same thing in gym terms: resistance training two to three days a week, 8 to 12 reps per set for most goals. Three 40-minute dumbbell sessions plus daily walks cover those recommendations in full.

How to choose dumbbell weight

The rule is simple: the weight is right when you finish the planned reps cleanly and the last two feel hard. On an effort scale that is a 7 or 8 out of 10, with two or three reps left in the tank. If you finish a set feeling you could do ten more, the weight is light. If technique falls apart on rep six of ten, the weight is heavy.

The problem with a single pair is that your legs and back are stronger than your shoulders. A weight that is hard for the dumbbell shoulder press will be light for the dumbbell deadlift. You have three ways out: adjustable dumbbells, two pairs, or making the movement harder without adding weight through slow tempo, pauses at the bottom, and single-arm or single-leg variations. Forma handles this differently: you enter which dumbbells you own, the program describes loads as light, medium or heavy, and the app maps those labels to your set.

If you have lower-back or shoulder problems or high blood pressure, talk to a doctor before starting a strength program and begin with your lightest pair.

Full body dumbbell workout at home: the session

The session takes 35 to 40 minutes. Every one of the dumbbell exercises at home listed here lives in the Forma library on video, with technique cues.

Warm-up, two rounds

  • Jog in place, 45 seconds.
  • Arm circles, 30 seconds.
  • Squat to stand, 6 reps.
  • World’s greatest stretch, 2 reps per side.

Strength, three supersets

Each pair is done for three sets: do the first exercise, go straight into the second, then rest 60 to 90 seconds.

SupersetExercise 1Exercise 2Rest
AGoblet squat × 10Dumbbell floor press × 1060–90 s
BDumbbell deadlift × 10Bent-over row × 8 per side60–90 s
CShoulder press × 8Dumbbell reverse lunge × 10 (5 per leg)60 s

Do the first set of superset A with a light weight; that is your specific warm-up.

Finisher: AMRAP 8 minutes

6 dumbbell thrusters, 8 push-ups, 10 sit-ups. Cycle through for eight minutes, resting in short breaks. Record rounds and reps. What AMRAP means and how to pace it is covered in the guide to workout formats.

Cool-down

Hamstring stretch 30 seconds per leg, hip flexor stretch 30 seconds per leg, child’s pose 45 seconds.

How the app scales the session

Before you start, Forma offers three options. “Easier” cuts reps by roughly 15 percent and lengthens the rest, “harder” adds reps and shortens the breaks, “as planned” keeps the coach’s numbers. The warm-up and cool-down are never scaled. If your baseline test placed you at level 1, complex movements are swapped for simpler ones down the exercise’s own chain: the thruster becomes a front squat or a goblet squat, the floor press becomes a push-up, the deadlift becomes a glute bridge. After the session you rate the effort from 1 to 10 and log how much you completed, and the next session gets slightly easier or harder.

Common mistakes

  • Too heavy on day one. A dumbbell workout for beginners is built on technique, not kilograms. Keep the effort at 6 or 7 out of 10 for the first two weeks.
  • A rounded back on the deadlift and row. The spine stays neutral and the movement comes from the hips. If your back rounds, the weight is too heavy or the dumbbells are too far from your shins.
  • Arching the lower back on the press. Squeeze the glutes, brace the abs, keep the ribs down. If you cannot press without arching, go lighter.
  • Arms only. Biceps curls do not replace squats and deadlifts. A dumbbell complex for the whole body is built around the legs and back; the arms get their share in presses and rows.
  • Untimed rest. Ninety seconds between sets quietly becomes five minutes on your phone. Use a timer; the app has one built in.

How to progress

  1. Weeks 1 and 2. Three sessions a week with a rest day in between. Medium weight, reps as in the table. The goal is to learn the movements and remember what each weight feels like.
  2. Weeks 3 and 4. Double progression: keep the weight and add one rep per set until you reach 12. When you hit 12 in all three sets, increase the weight and drop back to 8 to 10 reps. In week 4 cut the volume by about a third; that is a deload, not weakness.
  3. Weeks 5 to 8. Move to harder variants of the same pattern: goblet squat to front squat, shoulder press to push press, bent-over row to renegade row, deadlift to dumbbell clean. Add a 40-second farmer carry at the end of the strength block.
  4. After that. When the pair is light for every movement and your technique is stable, either buy more weight or move to a kettlebell; see the guide to kettlebell workouts at home.

The sign it is time to go harder: two consecutive sessions rated 6 out of 10 or lower with the full plan completed. That is exactly how Forma decides whether to recommend the “harder” option.

Wrap-up

A full body dumbbell workout at home works when it contains the five patterns, an honestly chosen weight and a clear progression rule. Three 40-minute sessions a week satisfy the WHO and ACSM guidance on strength training and produce a visible result within two months. If you would rather build the base without weights first and let the volume adjust to you automatically, open the Start course: twenty short sessions on a mat and a chair — the dumbbells then land on technique that is already there.

FAQ

What dumbbell weight should I use at home? +

Pick a weight you can press overhead for 10 clean reps with the last two feeling hard. That same pair will be light for squats and deadlifts, which is why adjustable dumbbells or two pairs are worth having.

How many times a week should I do a full body dumbbell workout? +

Three sessions a week with a rest day between them works for most people. The ACSM recommends resistance training for the major muscle groups on at least two days a week.

Can you train the whole body with one pair of dumbbells? +

Yes, as long as the program includes a squat, a deadlift, a press, a row and a lunge. When the pair gets light, make the movement harder instead: slow the tempo, add pauses, go single-arm or single-leg.

Do I need to warm up before dumbbell training? +

Yes: five to seven minutes of easy cardio, arm circles and bodyweight squats. Treat the first set of each exercise as a warm-up set with a light weight.

How long should I rest between sets? +

Rest 60 to 90 seconds between sets in the strength blocks; in the finisher, rest in short breaks as needed. If technique breaks down, rest longer rather than lowering the quality.
