You want AMRAP, EMOM, For Time and Tabata explained in plain words, because the same squats and burpees feel like four different workouts depending on the label above them. That is the point. A format is a rule set: how much time you have, who controls the pace, you or the clock, and what counts as the score. Once you understand the rules you stop guessing the effort and start managing it. This guide covers the four core CrossFit workout formats from a coach's perspective, with four ready-to-run sessions you can do at home with no equipment.

## AMRAP, EMOM, For Time and Tabata explained side by side

All four formats are ways of packaging the same movements into different work-to-rest relationships. CrossFit workout formats differ not in the exercises but in what is held fixed: time, volume or intensity.

| Format | Rule | What is fixed | Score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AMRAP | Max rounds in a set time | Time | Rounds + reps |
| EMOM | Set work at the top of each minute | Volume and time | Completed or not, quality |
| For Time | Set volume as fast as possible | Volume | Time to finish |
| Tabata | 8 × (20 s work / 10 s rest) | Time and intervals | Reps in the worst interval |

Keep that table in mind whenever you read a workout. It tells you immediately where you get to distribute your effort and where the timer does it for you.

## AMRAP: as many rounds as possible

AMRAP gives you a time window, say 12 minutes, and a short circuit of two to four movements. You cycle through the circuit until the clock stops. Resting is allowed, but every pause costs you rounds.

What is AMRAP really training? Pacing. The classic failure is a sprint through the first two minutes followed by a collapse in the fifth. A well-run AMRAP looks boring from the outside: the same pace from the first round to the last, hard but controlled breathing, technique that does not fall apart.

### How to score it

Write down full rounds plus the reps of the unfinished round, for example "6 rounds + 8 reps". This is exactly how Forma records an AMRAP block and compares it with your previous attempts. Without the number, the format loses its main advantage, which is that it is measurable.

## EMOM: every minute on the minute

At the top of every minute you complete a set amount of work, then rest for whatever is left. Twelve squats in 25 seconds means 35 seconds of rest. Twelve squats in 50 seconds means 10 seconds of rest and a harder next minute.

An EMOM workout is the safest format for anyone new to intense work. The volume is decided in advance, the rest is built in, and the timer stops you from both slacking and overreaching. It is also the best format for practising technique under fatigue: one movement, many repetitions, short breaks.

### How much work per minute

Aim for a task that takes 30 to 40 seconds so you keep at least 20 seconds of rest. If you are still working when the next minute starts, the volume is too high. That is not a failure, it is data: cut two or three reps next time.

## For Time: a fixed task, done as fast as you can

For Time sets the volume and asks for the fastest finish. The classic scheme is 21-15-9: 21 reps of each movement, then 15, then 9. Your score is the time on the clock. Almost every for time workout has a time cap; if you do not make it, you record the work you completed.

This format produces the strongest stimulus and the strongest temptation to trade technique for seconds. The rule is simple: any rep you would not count fresh does not count tired either.

## Tabata: 20 seconds on, 10 seconds off

Tabata is a protocol of eight intervals of 20 seconds of work and 10 seconds of rest, four minutes in total. It is named after the researcher Izumi Tabata, whose group studied very short, very intense intervals in trained athletes. The key word is intensity: 20 seconds implies near-maximal effort, so the movement has to be simple and safe at speed.

Tabata at home works well with [high knees](exercise:high_knees), [jumping jacks](exercise:jumping_jack) and [mountain climbers](exercise:mountain_climber). Score it by the worst interval: 12 reps in the first and 5 in the eighth means your score is 5. That rule forces an even pace instead of an early explosion.

## Four home workouts, one per format

One session per format, all built from the Forma library. Warm up first: a minute of [jogging in place](exercise:jog_in_place), [arm circles](exercise:arm_circles) and ten [squat-to-stands](exercise:squat_to_stand).

**AMRAP 12 minutes.** 5 [burpees](exercise:burpee), 10 [push-ups](exercise:push_up), 15 [air squats](exercise:air_squat). Aim for an even pace and rest in 5 to 10 second sips, not one long break.

**EMOM 12 minutes, three stations.** Minute 1: 12 air squats. Minute 2: 8 push-ups. Minute 3: 30 seconds of mountain climbers. Four rounds. Each task should take no more than 40 seconds.

**For Time, 12-minute cap.** 21-15-9 of [jump squats](exercise:jump_squat), push-ups and [sit-ups](exercise:sit_up). Record your time; if you hit the cap, record how much you finished.

**Tabata, 4 minutes.** Eight intervals of high knees. Count the reps in every interval; the lowest number is your score.

How the app scales these sessions. Before you start, Forma offers three choices: "easier" trims the volume by roughly 15 percent and lengthens the rest, "harder" adds volume and shortens the breaks, "as planned" keeps the coach's numbers. The clock on AMRAP, EMOM and Tabata never changes, only the work inside it, and For Time keeps its cap. If your test put you at level 1, burpees become [half burpees](exercise:half_burpee) and push-ups become [knee push-ups](exercise:knee_push_up). After the session you rate the effort from 1 to 10, and the next session gets slightly easier or harder based on that rating and how much of the plan you completed.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting at maximum.** In AMRAP and For Time the first two minutes should feel too easy. If they do not, you have already lost the finish.
- **Trading technique for seconds.** Half squats and push-ups with a sagging lower back do not count. Slow down or cut the volume.
- **Overloading the EMOM.** Less than 20 seconds of rest per minute is the signal to shrink the task.
- **Tabata on complex movements.** Burpees and jumping lunges at near-maximal speed are a poor idea for most people. Use simple cyclical movements.
- **Not writing the score down.** A format without a number is just fatigue. Record rounds, time or the worst interval.
- **Going hard every day.** The WHO recommends 150 to 300 minutes of moderate activity a week, and most of it should be moderate, not maximal. Keep intense formats to two or three days a week with walking and easy work in between. If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure or have not trained in a long time, talk to a doctor before adding near-maximal intervals.

## How to progress

1. **Weeks 1 and 2.** One intense format a week; everything else is EMOM with generous rest and technique work. Learn to hold an even pace.
2. **Weeks 3 and 4.** Two interval days: AMRAP and For Time on separate days with at least 48 hours between them. Repeat one workout and compare the numbers.
3. **Month 2.** Add Tabata as a short finisher after a strength block rather than as a stand-alone session. Increase volume by 5 to 10 percent at a time, never double it.
4. **The sign it is time to go harder:** two consecutive sessions rated 6 out of 10 or lower with the full plan completed. That is the same rule Forma uses to recommend the "harder" option.

## Wrap-up

AMRAP teaches pacing, EMOM teaches technique under a timer, For Time teaches grit, and Tabata teaches short, even, maximal output. None of them is better than the others; each has a job, and a good program rotates them.
